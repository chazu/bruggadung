i want to make a maximalist roguelike. give me some ideas for what that might entail

Oh, I love this idea. Especially because roguelikes are one of the few genres where you can pursue absolute, unreasonable complexity without necessarily requiring a massive art budget.

But I think there are several very different interpretations of a maximalist roguelike, and some are much more interesting than simply having 10,000 items and 400 character classes.

My favorite interpretation would be:

A game in which practically everything is simulated, everything has consequences, and the player is constantly discovering that systems interact in ways nobody anticipated.

Think NetHack meets Dwarf Fortress meets Caves of Qud, with some of the obsessive interface complexity of an old flight simulator.

Let's explore the possibilities.
1. Six flavors of maximalism
The Best Traditional Roguelike Games | Den of Geek

1. Systemic maximalism

Everything interacts with everything. Heat, pressure, moisture, disease, chemistry, combustion, structural integrity, electrical conductivity, acoustics, smell, and fluid dynamics all matter.

A fire doesn't merely damage you. It consumes oxygen, creates smoke, heats nearby objects, weakens supports, and potentially changes the dungeon's airflow.

2. Combinatorial maximalism

Ridiculous numbers of character builds, equipment components, spells, crafting recipes, materials, mutations, professions, and skills.

You can become a fungal necromancer who specializes in clockwork prosthetics and contract law. And that's a mechanically sensible character.

3. Social maximalism

Every NPC has relationships, obligations, possessions, economic interests, prejudices, memories, schedules, and institutional affiliations.

Kill a shopkeeper and you don't merely lose a merchant. You disrupt a supply chain, create an inheritance dispute, and possibly trigger a municipal investigation.

4. Interface maximalism

The interface itself becomes an elaborate instrument: multiple panels, nested menus, keyboard shortcuts, logs, inspectable diagrams, maps, dashboards, reference manuals, and specialized consoles.

Different professions might even have completely different interfaces to the same world.

5. World maximalism

Instead of generating a dungeon, generate an entire civilization and its history: geography, religion, languages, wars, trade networks, dynasties, ruins, migration, and mythology.

The dungeon is a physical consequence of that history, not merely a stack of randomly generated rooms.

6. Ontological maximalism

My favorite weird one: the game's simulation isn't limited to physical objects. Laws, rumors, promises, religious beliefs, bureaucracies, ownership, and identities are modeled as first-class things.

You can steal someone's sword, but perhaps you can also steal their legal identity, inherit their debts, or acquire authority over their ancestral ghost.
2. Some actual game concepts

Here are five settings that would justify going completely overboard.
The Infinite City

A procedurally generated city containing millions of inhabitants and centuries of accumulated history.

Every building is enterable. Every institution operates. Every NPC has a place in society. There's an economy, a legal system, municipal infrastructure, organized crime, public transit, utilities, and local politics.

The player begins as an absolutely insignificant person.

The twist: There is no separate dungeon. The city itself is the dungeon.

You might spend an entire run exploring the plumbing beneath one university, exposing a counterfeiting operation, or working your way through the ranks of a municipal bureaucracy.

You can become a detective, revolutionary, con artist, landlord, archaeologist, assassin, or sanitation inspector.

    Artifacts have actual provenance and histories.

    Languages change over time.

    Factions exploit ancient infrastructure they barely understand.

    Monsters possess ecologies and evolutionary histories.

A roguelike set entirely aboard an enormous, malfunctioning generation ship.

A wilderness survival roguelike where the ecology is simulated with almost absurd fidelity.

Creatures eat, reproduce, compete, migrate, become diseased, and alter their environments.

3. The mechanic I'd build the entire game around

I think the most interesting possible direction is universal interaction.

Most roguelikes have many objects but comparatively few meaningful verbs. Something is edible, equippable, throwable, or usable, and the possibilities end there.

Instead, give objects general physical and semantic properties, then define operations in terms of those properties.

4. Maximalist character creation

I'd go much further than race/class/attributes.

Imagine having a character with:

    Anatomy: individual limbs, organs, senses, and physiological conditions.

    Psychology: fears, compulsions, habits, values, memories, and addictions.

    Social identity: family, citizenship, professional credentials, reputation, titles, debts, and affiliations.

    Knowledge: languages, scientific disciplines, cultural practices, specialized technical knowledge.

    Physical skills: learned techniques rather than simple numerical bonuses.

    Possessions: including legal ownership and provenance.

    Relationships: favors owed, rivalries, secrets, romantic entanglements.

    Beliefs: religious, political, superstitious, and philosophical commitments.

Importantly, these categories should interact.

A character with damaged hearing might struggle in conversation but become better at reading lips. Their professional certification could allow them to access restricted areas. Their family connections might create obligations that interfere with an expedition.

You could even give the player the choice of a completely random life history rather than generating a build manually.
5. Information should be a simulated resource

Here's an aspect of maximalism I find especially compelling: the player shouldn't have perfect knowledge of the game state.

Most roguelikes show you exactly how much HP a monster has, what an item does after identification, and whether a door is locked.

A maximally simulated world should distinguish between:

    What is actually true.

    What your character can perceive.

    What your character believes.

    What other characters claim to be true.

    What is recorded in books, maps, and institutional databases.

You might purchase a map that is 80 years out of date.

An NPC might describe a monster inaccurately because they've only heard rumors.

A physician might misdiagnose an illness.

Your character could identify an alloy by its color, but be wrong because the object has been plated.

This would make investigation, observation, expertise, and documentation central gameplay systems.

And it would give your TUI an excuse to have magnificent reference screens, notebooks, diagnostic instruments, and epistemically qualified object inspectors.

7. The trick: maximize interactions, not just systems

There is one design principle I'd insist upon.

Suppose you implement 30 intricate simulation systems, but they operate independently. You will have 30 expensive, complicated minigames.

The really interesting quantity is the number of meaningful interactions between those systems.

Consider this single emergent sequence:

    A severe storm floods part of the city.
    Floodwater contaminates the municipal drinking supply.
    A disease outbreak overwhelms local hospitals.
    Medicine prices rise, incentivizing smuggling.
    A religious group blames the outbreak on forbidden technology.
    Riots disrupt the electrical grid.
    The player exploits the blackout to rob an archive.

Not one of those events needs to be a scripted quest.

That's the kind of maximalism I'd pursue: a world in which ordinary causal chains produce outrageous adventures.
8. How I'd keep it implementable

The temptation will be to simulate absolutely everything at maximum detail, all the time. That's probably fatal to the project.

Instead, I'd borrow a principle from simulation games: variable resolution.

Scale


Simulation

Immediate vicinity


Individual objects, agents, detailed physics, local interactions

Neighborhood


Buildings, businesses, households, traffic, infrastructure

City


Supply chains, public health, economic activity, faction influence

World


Migration, trade, history, wars, ecological changes

When something becomes relevant to the player, it can be instantiated in greater detail. Otherwise, it remains an aggregate model.

This is particularly compatible with your Dewdrop idea of deferring subcomponent generation until it's actually needed.

And for software architecture, I'd consider a small number of general foundations:

    An entity/property system capable of describing practically anything.

    A relation system for ownership, identity, membership, causation, and spatial containment.

    A unified process/event model for things changing over time.

    An affordance system to determine what operations are possible.

    A perception and knowledge system distinguishing reality from belief.

    A procedural generation system capable of expanding abstract things into concrete details.

That gives you something like a little simulation language, with a roguelike serving as its most elaborate application.

PALIMPSEST

A maximalist roguelike about being a very small person in an impossibly complicated world.

Here's my proposal: take the simulation ambition of Dwarf Fortress, the combinatorial absurdity of NetHack, the character possibilities of Caves of Qud, and the intricate procedural worldbuilding of a tabletop RPG—and attempt to make all of it into one coherent game.

But I want to add something distinctive: the game's most important mechanics won't be combat mechanics.

They'll be relationships between things.

A sword is a weapon, a piece of metal, a valuable commodity, a historical artifact, a stolen possession, a legal liability, and perhaps an object of religious veneration. All of those properties should be mechanically meaningful.

And the game world should be so rich that the player can invent goals the developer never thought to provide.
1. The premise

Palimpsest takes place in an enormous procedurally generated city.

The city is ancient, layered, overcrowded, politically unstable, and physically falling apart. Nobody knows how large it is. Nobody agrees on its history.

New buildings are constructed inside old buildings. Entire neighborhoods have been buried. Rivers have been redirected through underground infrastructure. Generations of inhabitants have left behind technologies, religions, legal codes, and inexplicable machinery.

There are thousands of professions, organizations, institutions, subcultures, and ecological niches.

The world has some supernatural phenomena, but they obey discoverable rules. Magic is another branch of natural philosophy, practiced through instruments, experiments, learned techniques, and occasionally bureaucratic paperwork.
The fundamental premise

You are born into this world, with a particular body, history, social position, set of abilities, and personal circumstances.

You are not chosen. You are not special.

You can become special.

You can attempt to become a legendary swordsman, industrial magnate, occult scholar, criminal mastermind, labor organizer, explorer, revolutionary, or simply the most accomplished locksmith in the city.

There is no required destiny.

And yes, you can die from tetanus while trying to repair a sewer gate.

4. Objects should be ridiculously expressive

This is arguably the most important subsystem.

Rather than generating thousands of items with arbitrary special effects, give everything a rich composition of properties and capabilities.

A mundane object might have:

    Material composition

    Geometry, mass, dimensions

    Mechanical characteristics

    Thermal and electrical properties

    Structural components

    Condition and damage

    Ownership and provenance

    Manufacturer and quality

    Cultural or religious significance

    Functional affordances

    Contents and spatial relationships

A pocket watch, for example, is composed of gears, springs, an escapement, hands, a casing, and a crystal.

An expert watchmaker can dismantle it.

A thief can alter its identifying engravings.

An engineer can repurpose the spring.

An investigator can use its stopped hands to infer the approximate time of an accident, provided they know how the watch was damaged.

A merchant can recognize the manufacturer's hallmark.

A supernatural practitioner might discover that its repeated motion gives it unusual ritual properties.

I want players to be able to discover uses for objects that aren't listed in a conventional item description.

The really interesting part is that tools, materials, knowledge, and actions all participate in the same system.

You might be unable to repair a machine because you don't understand it. Or because you lack a tool. Or because the replacement component isn't available. Or because the machine is under pressure and operating on it would kill you.

Each is a different obstacle, and each admits a different solution.
5. Make the city a living machine
Свиток любви и ярости

I'd treat the city as a collection of interdependent networks.

Each building is simultaneously:

    A physical structure.

    A location containing objects.

    A consumer and producer of resources.

    A workplace, residence, or institution.

    A node in transportation and utility networks.

    A place with historical, economic, and social significance.

Consider a tannery.

It employs workers, buys animal hides, consumes water and chemicals, produces leather, emits unpleasant smells, discharges waste, pays taxes, and sells goods to workshops.

Its employees have homes, families, schedules, and relationships.

Its owner might bribe inspectors to overlook pollution.

Downstream, a neighborhood experiences contaminated drinking water.

This could produce disease, political unrest, litigation, or an opportunity for extortion.

The simulation should produce these consequences whether or not the player happens to be watching.
A procedural city with history

World generation should work backward from the present.

First, generate a historical sequence of settlements, technologies, political changes, construction projects, economic booms, disasters, and cultural transformations.

Then derive the present-day city's physical and social organization from that history.

This generates things like:

    An abandoned subway built for a transportation system that no longer exists.

    A monastery converted into a municipal records office.

    A neighborhood where property ownership is disputed because two legal systems recognize different deeds.

    A guild whose monopoly derives from a 600-year-old royal decree.

    A sewer network whose builders never anticipated the weight of the buildings above it.

You get both an explorable environment and a coherent reason for its existence.

7. Magic should have a discoverable internal science

I wouldn't use a conventional spell list.

Instead, supernatural effects should emerge from a small number of underlying laws.

I'd also make magical knowledge subject to experimentation. Books can contain incorrect theories; different traditions can describe the same underlying phenomenon in incompatible terms.

8. An example of actual gameplay

Let's follow Marius, our disgraced pressure engineer.
The Blackwater Incident
Day 27

An unscripted sequence illustrating the simulation

    A strange observation

    Marius notices that water pressure in his apartment is unusually low.

    His engineering knowledge allows him to recognize that the fluctuation is inconsistent with normal demand.

    Investigation

    He visits a public pumping station.

    His professional credentials are suspended, so the attendant denies him access.

    Marius learns from an acquaintance that a maintenance crew has been working on a nearby aqueduct. He bribes a junior technician for a copy of the work order.

    A technical discovery

    The work order specifies replacement valves made from an alloy incompatible with the system's operating conditions.

    Marius suspects that the contractor has substituted cheaper components.

    He can attempt to expose the fraud, exploit it, or ignore it.

    A dangerous opportunity

    Marius decides to investigate the installed equipment.

    He obtains a maintenance uniform, enters the aqueduct through an access shaft, and discovers extensive corrosion.

    While inspecting a valve, he accidentally dislodges a damaged fitting.

    Water begins escaping under pressure.

    An escalating emergency

    The escaping water erodes the soil supporting an adjacent building.

    Nearby residents hear the noise. A maintenance worker attempts to isolate the affected pipe.

    The isolation valve jams.

    Marius knows enough to improvise a temporary repair, but doing so requires tools he doesn't have.

    Consequences

    Marius can flee, attempt a dangerous repair, summon help, or sabotage another section of the system to relieve pressure.

    The decisions have physical, economic, legal, and social consequences.

What makes this interesting is that none of it needs to be a hand-authored quest.

The contractor bought cheaper materials because of economic pressures. The material properties created an engineering failure. The failure changed the physical world.

Marius noticed the problem because of his expertise.

His opportunities arose from existing relationships, institutional rules, and the physical layout of the infrastructure.

I want the game to produce situations like that routinely.

And sometimes the player should simply hear about a major incident in the newspaper three days afterward.

11. The interface should expose complexity progressively

Despite my enthusiasm for ridiculous simulation depth, I wouldn't make every turn require navigating six menus.

The ordinary actions—move, examine, pick up, talk, attack, interact—should remain quick.

Complexity is something the player opens when they need it.

The game could have three modes of interaction:

    Immediate: Conventional roguelike keyboard commands.

    Contextual: Inspect an object and obtain a menu of available actions.

    Specialized: Open a dedicated interface for engineering, medicine, investigation, administration, or another complicated activity.

For example, examining a broken mechanical pump might expose Diagnose, Dismantle, Repair, Test, and Inspect Components.

Choosing Diagnose opens a detailed engineering interface.

Your expertise determines what information is available, how accurately it's presented, and what operations your character can perform.

I'd give the game an elaborate set of specialized interfaces, but make them discoverable through a consistent command grammar.

Your TurboVision-style F-key preferences would fit this beautifully.
12. Roguelike structure: lives, ambitions, and consequences

There's an interesting problem here: traditional roguelikes provide a clear objective and endpoint.

Palimpsest would be more open-ended.

My solution would be a combination of personal ambitions and world-level crises.
Personal ambitions

Characters can pursue goals such as:

    Accumulate enough wealth to purchase an estate.

    Become head of a professional guild.

    Discover the cause of a historical catastrophe.

    Manufacture an unprecedented invention.

    Found a religious order.

    Become mayor of a district.

    Establish a criminal organization.

    Discover a route into a legendary buried city.

Each ambition can have an ending or achievement condition, but no ambition is mandatory.
World-level crises

The city periodically experiences major systemic disruptions:

    Epidemics

    Famines

    Financial panics

    Political revolutions

    Infrastructure collapses

    Wars

    Supernatural anomalies

    Ecological catastrophes

These events are not necessarily centered on the player.

An inexperienced character might be forced to survive the consequences of a civil war.

An influential character might be able to start one.
Permadeath, with persistent history

I'd strongly consider preserving world history across successive characters.

Your character dies, but their actions survive.

A subsequent character might encounter a bridge you built, a scientific paper you published, an institution you founded, or the descendants of someone you murdered.

This would produce a wonderfully elaborate historical simulation, although I'd preserve the option of generating a completely fresh world for each run.

The key is that a dead character becomes part of the setting rather than a mere entry in a high-score table.
13. The architecture that could make this possible

Now here's the part I suspect you'll find especially interesting, given your ongoing work with typed feature structures, CUE, Logtalk, and Dewdrop.

I think the simulation should be built around a very small number of universal concepts.

Variable-resolution simulation

We'd also need to avoid simulating every molecule and every citizen all the time.

I would use different resolutions depending on relevance.

    Active simulation: Nearby people, individual objects, local machinery, tactical combat.

    Regional simulation: Neighborhood economies, utility networks, disease spread, faction activity.

    Aggregate simulation: Citywide production, population changes, trade, politics.

    Historical simulation: Long-term generational changes.

As the player approaches a location, aggregate state can be expanded into detailed entities.

This needs careful bookkeeping so that generated details remain consistent with prior history and observations.

Your Dewdrop deferred-generation approach is an obvious fit: generate subcomponents only when they become causally relevant or inspectable, while preserving the constraints already established.

The next design question I'd tackle is the most fundamental one: what is the universal action model? If we can figure out how objects, agents, knowledge, intentions, and affordances combine to make actions possible, we can start building the simulation outward from a surprisingly small core.
Okay well how do we determine the universal action model?

I think we should approach this almost like designing a small programming language.

The question isn't really "What actions can the player perform?"

It's:

What is the smallest set of concepts from which we can derive every meaningful action in the game?

And here's the important part: we shouldn't try to invent that set from first principles alone. We should derive it from a collection of radically different gameplay situations, looking for the common structure.

I have a candidate model, but I'd want to test it before committing to it.
1. Start with a definition of action

My proposed definition:

    An action is an intentional attempt by an agent to produce a change in the world, subject to constraints, over some interval of time.

That gives us several distinct concepts:

    Agent: Who is attempting the action?

    Intention: What outcome are they trying to achieve?

    Operation: What are they actually attempting to do?

    Participants: What entities are involved?

    Constraints: Under what circumstances can the operation succeed?

    Execution: What happens while the action takes place?

    Consequences: What changes in the world?

    Observations: Who learns about those changes?

The distinction between intention and consequence is crucial.

If I try to open a valve, the valve may be seized. If I try to repair it, I may accidentally break it. If I try to persuade someone, they may become suspicious.

The player controls attempts, not outcomes.

I would represent an attempted action roughly as:
A=(actor,operation,roles,parameters)
A=(actor,operation,roles,parameters)

Where roles binds entities to meaningful positions such as target, instrument, recipient, or destination.

The surrounding world state determines how that attempt resolves.
2. Separate three kinds of possibility

This is probably the single most important design decision.

We need to distinguish:

Question


Example

Is it physically possible?


Can I turn this valve with my available strength?

Is it institutionally permitted?


Am I authorized to operate this valve?

Do I believe it's possible?


Do I think this valve is operational?

These are independent.

A locked door might be physically breakable but legally prohibited to enter.

A forged credential might convince a guard that entry is authorized even though the credential has no actual legitimacy.

A character might attempt to operate a machine without knowing it's broken.

And a completely legitimate action might be physically impossible.

This means we shouldn't define affordances simply as actions that the game knows will succeed.

An affordance should be something more like:

    An operation an agent can reasonably attempt, given their capabilities, perceptions, and knowledge.

The resolver then determines what actually happens.

This produces a nice distinction between three queries:
can_attempt(actor, action)
can_succeed(world, action)
is_authorized(institution, action)

The first is mostly about the actor's perceived options.

The second is about actual world constraints.

The third is about normative or institutional rules.

I would be extremely careful never to collapse those together.

5. The world model determines which actions exist

Here's where your interest in typed feature structures becomes particularly relevant.

Suppose entities have structured descriptions of their capabilities and constraints.

A valve might be described as:
valve:
    kind: flow_control_device

    parts:
        handle:
            rotatable: true
            torque_required: 12 Nm

        body:
            material: bronze
            pressure_rating: 800 kPa

    state:
        opening: 0.0
        condition: 0.65

    connections:
        inlet: pipe_a
        outlet: pipe_b

And an agent might have:
agent:
    capabilities:
        grasp: true
        apply_torque:
            maximum: 30 Nm

    knowledge:
        mechanical_engineering: 0.7

An operation schema describes the requirements for rotating something.
operation rotate:
    actor: entity
    target: rotatable_component
    parameters:
        angle: number

    constraints:
        - actor can reach target
        - actor can manipulate target
        - target permits rotation

    execution:
        - apply torque
        - resolve mechanical response
        - advance time

The operation doesn't need to know what a valve is.

It only needs a target with appropriate capabilities.

The valve's structure determines what rotation means mechanically.

For a valve, it changes flow resistance.

For a clock, it may wind a spring.

For a door, it changes the orientation of the door or its handle.

This is a powerful form of polymorphism.

Rather than giving every object a list of hardcoded interactions, we derive possible operations by matching the requirements of operation schemas against entity properties.

Affordances become a query over the world model.

That is very close to typed feature structure unification.
6. Effects should produce events, not directly invoke every other system

I'd make events the central integration mechanism.

Suppose the player opens a valve.

The mechanical operation updates the valve's opening and emits a ValveAdjusted event.

That may produce a PressureChanged event.

The structural simulation might determine that a damaged pipe fails.

This is also a nice fit for deterministic replay and debugging.
7. Model ongoing processes separately from actions

A significant portion of the game world changes without anyone intentionally doing anything.

Examples:

    Fire spreads.

    Steel corrodes.

    Water flows.

    People become hungry.

    Diseases progress.

    Businesses manufacture goods.

    Organizations accumulate debt.

    Knowledge is forgotten.

These are processes, not actions.

An action can initiate, interrupt, or modify a process.

Lighting a fire is an action.

Combustion is a process.

Opening a valve is an action.

Fluid flow is a process.

Signing an employment contract is an action.

Performing the work, earning wages, and accruing obligations happen over time.

8. Social actions need another layer: institutional facts

I think this is where many attempts at universal simulation models become awkward.

Consider the action Transfer ownership.

Ownership isn't a physical property of an object.

It is a relationship recognized by some social system.

Suppose Alice gives Bob a watch.

Physically, the watch changes possession.

But has ownership changed?

That depends.

    Did Alice own the watch?

    Was the transfer intended as a gift?

    Did Bob accept it?

    Does the applicable legal system recognize the transfer?

    Was Alice acting under coercion?

The physical transfer and the legal transfer are different events.

We need to model institutional facts: facts that exist because a particular organization or community recognizes certain rules and procedures.

For example:
institution: Municipal Court

rule:
    valid_property_transfer:
        requires:
            - transferor owns property
            - transferor consents
            - recipient accepts
            - property is transferable

        establishes:
            - recipient owns property

A thief can physically possess a watch without owning it.

A fraudster can forge documents that convince someone they own it.

A court can issue an order changing the recognized legal owner without the watch moving physically.

And two jurisdictions can disagree.

This introduces a fascinating distinction:

World facts, institutional facts, and individual beliefs can all contradict one another without the simulation itself becoming inconsistent.

They are claims within different contexts.

That seems especially compatible with the Logtalk-like relational semantics you've been contemplating.
9. Knowledge needs to influence both available actions and their outcomes

We should avoid treating knowledge as a simple numeric skill bonus.

A person who understands a machine can recognize its components, predict consequences, and formulate meaningful plans.

An untrained person can still turn a handle.

They just might not understand what it does.

I would model knowledge as structured beliefs with provenance and confidence.

For example:
belief:
    holder: marius

    proposition:
        valve_17.controls: reservoir_3

    confidence: 0.85

    evidence:
        - old engineering diagram
        - personal observation

A character might learn something from:

    Direct observation

    Experimentation

    Instruction

    Reading

    Testimony

    Inference

This opens up a really interesting affordance distinction.

The world might permit an operation, but the character might not recognize that operation as useful.

An expert engineer sees a pressure-regulation mechanism.

A novice sees an unidentified assembly with a lever.

Both can pull the lever.

Only the engineer can predict the probable consequences.

I wouldn't universally prohibit actions based on missing knowledge. Instead, knowledge should affect what the character can identify, plan, perform reliably, and understand afterward.

What would count as success?

    Compositionality: New situations emerge by combining existing operations.

    Extensibility: New entity types mostly require data and domain rules, not new engine machinery.

    Causal intelligibility: We can explain why an action succeeded, failed, or produced a consequence.

    Locality: An action doesn't require evaluating the entire world graph.

That last one matters enormously for performance.

The system needs to identify the relevant entities, relations, and processes efficiently.
12. My proposed implementation strategy

Given your interests, I would be tempted by a three-part design.

A. Typed feature structures and constraints

Describe entities, operation signatures, materials, capabilities, and constraints.

This is the CUE-like part.

B. Relational inference and affordance discovery

Determine which entities satisfy roles, which operations are available, what institutional rules apply, and what relationships hold.

This is the Logtalk-like part.

C. Deterministic transition and process engine

Resolve attempted actions, advance time, update state, maintain invariants, and emit observable events.

This is the simulation-runtime part.

I would keep the transition engine deliberately explicit.

Not every physical simulation needs to be expressed as logical inference.

The hydraulic model can use numerical computation. Combat can use specialized mechanics. An economic model can use aggregate equations.

The unifying abstraction is the semantics of entities, operations, and events—not the demand that everything use the same implementation technique.

The typed structures describe the things. The relational rules determine what can apply. The transition engine makes things happen.
Where I think we should go next

I wouldn't finalize the primitive operations yet.

I'd first work through perhaps 20–30 representative situations and try to express each using the same provisional model. We should deliberately include situations that seem hard to represent:

    Persuading someone

    Making a promise

    Inventing a tool

    Teaching a skill

    Diagnosing a disease

    Declaring someone legally dead

    Operating a machine incorrectly

    Performing a religious ceremony

    Delegating authority

    Establishing a new institution

These will expose missing concepts much faster than straightforward inventory interactions.

And there's one architectural question I find especially important:

Should actions be described primarily as transformations of state, or as the satisfaction and violation of constraints?

My suspicion is that we want both, but with different responsibilities: constraints determine applicability and validity; transitions represent causal change.

If we get that division right, we could end up with something more interesting than a roguelike action system: a small, general-purpose language for modeling agents acting in a richly structured world.

3. Noita — a general substrate produces surprising interactions

Noita is fascinating because it achieves enormous combinatorial variety through relatively general physical rules.

A liquid doesn't need a special rule for every container. It flows because it's a liquid and the surrounding geometry permits it.

Consider a hypothetical combination:

    A spell breaks a container.

    Liquid spills onto the floor.

    Another effect heats that liquid.

    The liquid changes phase.

    The resulting vapor changes visibility or creates a hazard.

The limitation is that Noita's substrate is predominantly physical. It doesn't solve the harder problem of how contracts, beliefs, identities, and institutions might interact with physical events.

That's where Palimpsest needs to go beyond it.
4. Dwarf Fortress — shared underlying representations
More music of the dwarves - North Coast Synthesis Ltd.

Dwarf Fortress gets much of its depth from reusing information across systems.

Materials have properties. Creatures have anatomies. Objects are made of materials. Manufacturing jobs transform resources. History establishes relationships between people, civilizations, and artifacts.

Tarn Adams discussed making material reactions more data-driven, allowing materials to interact without individually programming every combination. His development logs also describe generalized material and tissue definitions used across creatures, manufactured goods, and production processes.
bay12games.com+1

A particularly valuable insight appears in an old developer discussion about chemical reactions.

Adams points out the computational danger of checking enormous numbers of possible reactions whenever objects touch.

That's exactly the risk we'd face with universal affordance discovery.

If there are 10,000 possible interactions, we cannot evaluate all 10,000 against every entity pair on every turn.

The engine needs to narrow the possibilities using material types, local conditions, indexed capabilities, and relevant events.

The other important characteristic of Dwarf Fortress is that actors have independent activities. Jobs, needs, and relationships generate situations without the player initiating every action.

Lesson: Give different simulations common representations, but keep execution localized and index operations by their requirements.
5. Space Station 13 — perhaps the most instructive example

I hadn't emphasized this in our initial discussion, but I think Space Station 13 deserves serious attention.

Space Station 13 is almost an existence proof for many of the interactions we want.

It has machinery, electrical systems, atmospherics, chemistry, medicine, social roles, access control, and elaborate chains of technical failures.

The systems are often quite specialized. A gas simulation doesn't use the same internal mechanics as a medical system.

Yet they interact through shared objects, machines, environmental state, and signals.
The most revealing implementation detail

The open-source /tg/station codebase documents its atmospheric simulation in unusual depth.

Pipes, gas mixtures, machines, and environmental tiles participate in a specialized simulation. A connected set of pipes can share an aggregate gas mixture, rather than representing every little pipe section as a completely independent simulation.

Inactive regions can stop receiving expensive updates until something changes.

The documentation explicitly emphasizes approximating the behavior that matters for gameplay, including selectively activating and sleeping portions of the simulation.

7. NetHack — the power of reusable verbs

8. RimWorld — emergence still benefits from deliberate pacing

RimWorld provides an important counterargument to pure simulationism.

The game simulates colonists' physical and psychological conditions, relationships, production, climate, combat, and other systems.

But it also has an AI storyteller that selects events based on the desired dramatic pacing. The developers explicitly describe it as a mechanism for constructing stories by controlling world events.

That suggests a useful distinction:

    The simulation determines what happens as a consequence of world state.

    A director can introduce plausible disturbances that create new opportunities or problems.

A more concrete architecture for Palimpsest

Here's what I would provisionally adopt.
A. Entities have components and capabilities

An object is an entity with an identity and a collection of properties and behaviors.

For example, a valve can be:
Entity: Valve #172

Components:
  PhysicalBody
  RotatableControl
  FlowRegulator
  WearAndDamage
  Ownership

Capabilities:
  rotate
  inspect
  dismantle
  transfer_fluid

The capabilities indicate operations the object can participate in.

They do not individually define every possible consequence.
B. Actions bind entities to roles

Suppose Marius attempts to operate Valve #172.
Action:
  operation: rotate

  actor: Marius
  target: Valve #172
  instrument: hand

  parameter:
    angle: 90 degrees

The action system identifies the appropriate operation schema and checks its requirements.

Some requirements are general: reachability, manipulation, time, force.

Others belong to the target's components: mechanical resistance, condition, permitted rotation range.

Authority and belief are evaluated separately from physical feasibility.
C. Domain systems resolve consequences

The mechanical system handles the rotation.

The fluid system responds to the resulting valve position.

The maintenance system updates wear if relevant.

The social system can recognize that Marius operated municipal equipment without authorization.

The perception system determines who noticed.

Each uses its own algorithms, but they operate on compatible world state.
D. Events preserve causal relationships

The engine records relevant events such as:
ValveOperated:
  actor: Marius
  target: Valve #172
  result: opened
  time: 09:42

PressureChanged:
  system: Southern Aqueduct
  cause: ValveOperated #5842
  previous: 740 kPa
  current: 810 kPa

These values are illustrative.

The event log doesn't need to retain every numerical integration step. It should preserve significant occurrences and enough provenance to explain important outcomes.

An observer's knowledge is derived from what they can perceive or subsequently learn—not from automatically receiving the authoritative event log.
The most important thing I'd change from our original proposal

Originally, I was leaning toward defining a universal action algebra and implementing the game in terms of those primitives.

After comparing these games, I'm less convinced that's the right starting point.

I think attempting to reduce everything to a small number of primitive verbs might become an exercise in artificial elegance.

There is probably no useful single low-level operation that encompasses tightening a bolt, persuading a witness, diagnosing pneumonia, and establishing a municipal corporation.

But all four have a common action structure:

    An actor

    A desired result

    Role-bound participants

    Capabilities and requirements

    An execution mechanism

    Costs and duration

    Possible failure

    State changes

    Observable consequences

So I'd make that structure universal while allowing domain-specific semantics.

Typed feature structures and unification would help describe and match capabilities. Relational rules would handle relationships and institutional constraints. Specialized algorithms would handle processes requiring physics, numerical methods, or complex procedural logic.

The whole thing would be held together by a common state and event model.

1. Cataclysm: Dark Days Ahead — capability-based requirements

Core idea: An action requires capabilities, not necessarily specific objects.

Cataclysm has an exceptionally detailed crafting, repair, and item-use system. Much of its content is declaratively defined in JSON.

The key abstraction is a tool quality.

For example, a tool might provide:

{
  "qualities": [
    ["SCREW", 1],
    ["HAMMER", 2]
  ]
}

This is a simplified example of the actual JSON format.

The numbers indicate quality levels. A task requiring SCREW level 1 can use a tool that provides that capability at the required level or better.

The important point is that the task doesn't have to know the tool's identity.

A screwdriver and a multitool might satisfy the same requirement.

Cataclysm's documentation also supports requirements involving specific tools, interchangeable alternatives, material components, skills, and proficiencies.
DDA Docs+1
How an action works

Consider crafting a knife.

    Find the recipe

    The recipe describes the desired product, material components, tools, skills, difficulty, and time required.

    Resolve requirements

    The engine checks available inventory and accessible resources.

    A recipe can accept alternative tools or require a tool with a particular quality.

    Account for expertise

    Character skills and proficiencies affect whether the task can be performed and how effectively.

    Some missing proficiencies make work slower or less successful rather than categorically impossible.

    Execute an activity

    Longer operations use an activity system with lifecycle methods for starting, processing turns, finishing, and cancellation.

    Produce a result

    The appropriate game logic resolves success, failure, material consumption, and output.

That fourth step is worth examining.

Cataclysm's activity system uses specialized activity actors. The documented lifecycle includes start, do_turn, finish, and canceled.

An activity actor can also serialize the information necessary to resume after loading a save.
docs.cataclysmdda.org

So Cataclysm separates at least three things:

    Requirements: What must be available?

    Activity: How does the operation occupy time?

    Effect: What actually happens?

This is much more expressive than a simple function that instantly changes an object.
What I would borrow

For Palimpsest, I'd generalize tool qualities into capabilities that can belong to objects, people, organizations, and environments.

For example:
Capability:
    name: apply_torque
    maximum: 30 Nm

Or:
Capability:
    name: authorize_entry
    jurisdiction: municipal_waterworks

An action can then specify the capabilities it requires.

The same matching machinery might support opening a valve, performing surgery, or signing a legally valid permit—even though the execution semantics would be quite different.

One caveat: authorization should remain a separate normative check. A character can physically forge a permit without having the authority to issue a genuine one.

The big insight: Cataclysm gives us a concrete model for determining whether the available entities can satisfy an operation's requirements.
2. Caves of Qud — component-based entities and event handling

Core idea: An object's behavior emerges from the components attached to it.

Qud is particularly useful because its official modding documentation exposes some of the actual implementation machinery.

Its objects are constructed from blueprints. A blueprint can inherit from another blueprint and specify components, properties, and tags.

Here's a simplified approximation of how an object might be composed:

    <part Name="Physics" />
    <part Name="Metal" />
    <part Name="Commerce" />
    <part Name="MeleeWeapon" />
</object>

This is illustrative, not a complete working Qud definition.

Each part can supply state and behavior. The game's documentation demonstrates this mechanism with weapons, ammunition, and mutations.

The interesting bit: parts receive events

A Qud part can register interest in particular events.

The official code examples use operations such as:

Object.RegisterPartEvent(this, "ProjectileHit");

When the registered event occurs, the part's handler can execute logic.

For example, a projectile-related component can respond to a hit by applying poison to its target.

A more revealing example: Flaming Hands

Qud's official mutation tutorial includes an implementation of a flaming-hands ability.

That single mutation participates in several different systems:

    It registers an activated ability.

    It contributes an option to the NPC's combat AI.

    It responds when the ability is used.

If we simply allow components to register arbitrary event handlers, complexity can become difficult to control. Components might change the same state, interfere with each other, or create surprising event cycles.

For Palimpsest, I'd put tighter boundaries around:

3. Space Station 13 — independently specialized simulation systems

Core idea: Different parts of the world have their own simulation models, connected through shared objects and interfaces.

This is probably my favorite of the four from an engineering perspective.

I'll focus on the open-source /tg/station implementation, because its source and technical documentation are available.

Unlike Qud's object-part approach, Space Station 13 has some substantial simulations built around entire domains rather than individual objects.

Its atmospheric simulation is a particularly instructive example.
How its atmosphere simulation works

The main concepts are:

Responsibility

Pumps, heaters, valves, vents, and other devices

Environmental tile


Contains local atmospheric state

Processing subsystem


Schedules the portions of the simulation needing updates

A room doesn't need to simulate the motion of every gas molecule.

Connected pipes can share a single gas mixture. Machinery can maintain its own mixtures and exchange material with the pipeline.

The implementation carefully controls the order of these exchanges so that different processing orders don't produce arbitrary differences.

The clever optimization: sleeping simulations

Here's something particularly relevant to Palimpsest.

In /tg/station, atmospheric tiles don't all receive full simulation updates forever.

When a region reaches a sufficiently stable state, it can stop active processing.

A disturbance wakes the relevant region.

The system also uses structures called excited groups to help equalize interacting regions.

Its documentation discusses these optimizations in detail, including the substantial implementation complexity they introduce.

Component changes state
        ↓
Relevant simulation becomes active
        ↓
Simulation updates affected region
        ↓
New changes propagate
        ↓
Region stabilizes
        ↓
Simulation goes idle

Imagine applying that to Palimpsest.

A district's water network doesn't need full hydraulic calculations while nothing is changing.

Opening a valve activates the affected network.

An electrical short activates the relevant circuit.

A warehouse fire activates local combustion and heat-transfer simulations.

The vast majority of the city can remain computationally quiet.
What I would borrow

I'd give each simulation domain a defined interface:

trait Simulation {
    fn affected_by(&self, change: &StateChange) -> bool;
    fn activate(&mut self, region: RegionId);
    fn advance(&mut self, world: &World, dt: Duration)
        -> Vec<StateChange>;
}

This is proposed Palimpsest code, not Space Station 13 code.

The hydraulic system can use network-flow equations.

The electrical system can use circuit models.

The biological system can use a disease progression model.

The economic system can use market transactions and aggregate production.

They all respond to changes in relevant state.

We would need a scheduler that orders or iterates coupled simulations carefully, especially where one domain feeds back into another.

The big insight: We can have highly specialized, efficient models without sacrificing a common conceptual world.
4. Shadows of Doubt — evidence, provenance, and beliefs

Core idea: Actions matter because their consequences can be discovered and connected later.

This one may be the most important for making Palimpsest feel like a living world.

Its developer, Cole Jefferies, published unusually detailed explanations of the game's early design.

The central challenge was generating murder mysteries without scripting the crimes.

An NPC needed to choose a victim, commit a murder, leave a body, and potentially be observed. Other citizens needed to discover the crime. The player needed to reconstruct what happened.

All of this depended on independent routines, physical evidence, and persistent information.

How I'd extend it

I'd distinguish four kinds of information:

    World state: What is actually true.

    History: What events occurred.

    Evidence: What physical or informational traces remain.

    Belief: What an agent thinks is true.

They are related but not interchangeable.

Suppose Marius sees a man entering a warehouse.

The world may record an entry event.

Marius acquires a memory of the observation.

Later, Marius tells an investigator that the man entered the warehouse.

The investigator acquires a belief based on Marius's testimony.

That doesn't make the proposition true merely because it was asserted.

If Marius lies, the investigator can hold a false belief while the authoritative world state remains unchanged.

The distinction is central to deception, investigation, and social gameplay.
Model claims with provenance

I would use a representation something like this:
Claim:
    proposition:
        entered(person_42, warehouse_7)

    asserted_by: marius
    asserted_at: day_27, 09:42

    source:
        type: eyewitness_testimony

    confidence: 0.8

This is our proposed representation.

A physical observation might be more specific:
Observation:
    observer: marius
    subject: person_42
    activity: entering
    location: warehouse_7

    modality: vision
    conditions:
        visibility: poor
        distance: 30 meters

The observation can support a claim.

But the confidence assigned to that claim depends on perception, expertise, memory, and interpretation.

And a different witness might report something contradictory.

Now imagine the investigator discovers a receipt indicating that the suspect was elsewhere.

We have the foundation for actual reasoning about evidence.
The scalability warning

Shadows of Doubt's developer has also discussed the memory costs of maintaining a simulated city in which individual people and objects might become relevant, especially because past events must remain discoverable.

This is a real constraint, not merely a hypothetical one.
ColePowered Games Ltd.

We probably wouldn't want to preserve every event forever.

I'd consider three levels of persistence:

    Significant historical events: retained indefinitely.

    Events with outstanding consequences or references: retained while relevant.

    Routine events: summarized or discarded once their durable effects and necessary evidence have been recorded.

A citizen needn't retain the exact sequence of every meal they've eaten.

But a witness's observation of a suspicious person may need to survive until its memory decays.

The big insight: The simulation should produce causal traces that other agents can discover, interpret, remember, and dispute.
Putting the four together

Let's return to our favorite example: Marius repairing an industrial valve.

Each of the four architectures contributes something different.
An emergent repair attempt

    Cataclysm

    Determine the requirements

    Marius wants to repair a damaged valve.

    The repair operation requires mechanical expertise, a suitable tool, replacement material, physical access, and sufficient time.

    The capability matcher determines whether the available resources satisfy those requirements.
    Caves of Qud

    Resolve the object's behavior

    The valve contains components for flow regulation, mechanical operation, damage, and ownership.

    The repair operation interacts with the damaged component.

    Depending on circumstances, it may restore operation, fail, or further damage the valve.
    Space Station 13

    Propagate physical consequences

    The valve's condition or position changes.

    The hydraulic network responds.

    Pressure changes propagate through connected pipes. A damaged connection may leak. Water flow may be restored or disrupted.
    Shadows of Doubt

    Preserve discoverable consequences

    The repair may leave tool marks, damaged parts, altered instrument readings, or a maintenance record.

    A nearby worker might witness Marius operating restricted equipment.

    Later, an investigator may reconstruct what happened using these traces.

Notice that the four systems are not competing explanations of the same thing.

They operate at different levels.

The capability system determines whether an operation's requirements can be satisfied.

The component system determines how individual entities participate.

The specialized simulations calculate the consequences.

The evidence system makes the resulting history available for discovery and reasoning.

There is one more distinction I'd insist on: an event is not necessarily an observation.

That's how you get a world where information has genuine mechanical significance.

I also think we should resist making all five interfaces equally sophisticated at first.

My preferred implementation sequence would be:

    Build capability matching and action execution.

    Introduce composable entity components.

    Add one domain simulation—probably fluid flow or heat.

    Add event provenance and basic agent observations.

    Demonstrate an NPC discovering and responding to a consequence of the player's action.

At that point, we'd have a small world where action, physical causality, and knowledge are genuinely connected.

And I suspect the resulting software might begin to resemble the foundation of your CUE-plus-Logtalk ideas: typed structures describing entities and capabilities, relational inference establishing what operations are applicable, and explicit processes governing changes over time.

The critical difference is that we'd have a concrete application forcing us to confront which abstractions are actually useful.

Of the four, I'd start by implementing Cataclysm-style capability matching. But I would design its interface around Qud-style components from day one. That would give us a compact, testable foundation without committing prematurely to a grand universal language.
